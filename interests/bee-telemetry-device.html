In short

A solar-powered "smart bottom board" that sits under a beehive. It logs how the colony is doing, and samples, archives and identifies the pollen the bees bring home, so a season of foraging can be laid against hive health, the weather and what's in bloom.

Telemetry. Load cells weigh the hive, which is the best stand-in for colony size, nectar flow and swarms. Sensors track temperature and humidity inside and out, a row of probes maps the winter cluster, a microphone logs sound bands, and the entrance counts bees in and out. It all goes into SQLite and gets graphed over time.

Pollen robot. About once an hour a trap grid swings in for a few minutes, then out again, because colonies don't like losing pollen. If anything fails, the grid parks open.

  1. One pellet drops into the funnel throat and stops on a gate. A camera photographs it in white and 405 nm light, and measures it.
  2. A skewer wire pins one side, and a moving wire slices the pellet down the middle.
  3. The free half drops onto clear tape. That's the archive, kept whole for DNA work.
  4. The skewer swings 90° so the cut face points down, and stamps 2 or 3 smears onto a streak of propylene glycol and ethanol. The fluid matches the refractive index of the grains, so they image clearly without dark halos. That's what the microscope reads.
  5. Cover film seals the tape while a soft roller squashes the dry archive half into a flat disc. The tape winds into a swappable cassette with a desiccant pack to keep the archive dry.

Everything that touches pollen is used once: fresh wire, fresh tape, and a waste bin that leaves with the cassette. A blank gap each night marks the days on the reel.

One cassette = one experiment. A cassette runs 4 to 6 weeks and ships to a lab with a SQLite export, so any sample can be checked by its frame number or sent for DNA metabarcoding.

Built in stages.

  • First, the pollen robot: slicing, the tape-reel archive and the microscope, working together on the bench and then on a hive. For identification I'm pretraining a computer-vision model on pollen from Pacific Northwest plants, then shrinking it as small as possible so it runs on the device's own boards and tags each record.
  • Then every sensor at once: weight, temperature and humidity, sound, traffic, weather, CO₂, a varroa mite camera and LoRa bloom cameras on nearby plants. They all go in together because each one is part of what's being studied: the pollen only means something next to the colony's state and what's in bloom.
  • Then tagged foragers: a handful of marked bees and an entrance camera that reads their tags. The study is below.
  • Then an observation hive that decodes waggle dances automatically.

The forager study. The question: when bees settle on one flower or several, is that each bee's own choice, or a decision the colony makes together?

  • Two sampling rates, swapped per bee. Every tagged bee spends a few days with her pollen collected on every trip and a few days on every fourth trip. Half the bees start on each rate, then they swap, with a day or two off in between (a crossover design). Each bee is compared with herself, and the swap shows whether being sampled more often changes where she forages.
  • Controls. Tagged bees that are never stripped, so the camera shows what normal foraging looks like.
  • Every trip logged. The camera records every trip for every tagged bee, sampled or not, so the only thing that differs between groups is pollen removal.
  • The whole hive alongside. The hourly random pellets from untagged bees show what the colony as a whole is bringing in. Those get laid against the telemetry: weight, brood temperature, sound and dances.

If tagged bees' choices track the colony's state (brood, dearth, dances) more than their own past choices, it's a collective decision. If each bee stays loyal to her own flowers whatever the hive is doing, it's personal.

Built for science, not profit. Designs, code and data will be open once it works on a hive.

What hooked me

I learned about studies tracking which plants hives prefer and when. The idea: a solar-powered "smart bottom board" that logs sound, temperature, humidity, traffic and weight into SQLite and graphs it over time, plus a pollen robot that takes a pellet about once an hour, archives it on a tape cassette, and (later) reads it with a built-in microscope and a tiny local model. Then pollen trends can be laid against hive health, weather, bloom and the seasons.

Doing it for science, not profit: open hardware, open code, open data once it works on a hive.

Who it's for

  • Pollen researchers, especially labs doing pollen DNA metabarcoding and floral-fidelity work. The device is designed to give them as much useful data as possible: per-pellet records, a DNA-ready archive, sampling effort and context, raw images, open export.
  • More on how labs identify pollen in .

Scope and phases

  • MVP fallback: automated pellet collection onto the cassette, read at home or in a lab. The built-in microscope and ID model is the real goal, added as a stage.
  • Phase 1: telemetry + pollen robot (random hourly pellets).
  • Phase 2: ~5 tagged focal bees + an entrance camera reading tags (needs a 30-40 W panel and a separate battery box). Crossover study: strip every 4th trip vs every trip, counterbalanced order, washout days, at least 3 bees per order, never-stripped controls, camera logs every trip.
  • Phase 3: observation hive with automatic waggle-dance decoding.
  • Extras: LoRa bloom camera nodes at forage patches (send a bloom index, not photos), varroa sticky-board camera, weather (wind, rain, light, pressure), lid/inspection switch, CO₂ (SCD41), frame vibration.
  • What I want to test: colony state (brood, dearth, dances) drives how mixed the pellets are, not just individual bee preference.

Where it lives

  • Not inside the hive. Bees build comb in any gap over ~9 mm, propolise anything smaller, and it's 35 °C and humid in there.
  • A smart bottom board under the hive. Bees walk in over the trap grid; pellets fall through bee-proof mesh into the mechanism.
  • Three zones: bee zone at the front (wood or ASA/PETG, no PLA), sealed mechanism zone (gasket, silica gel, lens heater), and a store-bought IP65 electronics box that slides out the back like a drawer. The cassette slides out the back too.
  • Tamper-proofing for hives in public places: security screws, cable lock, panel high on its post. Condensation is the biggest risk.

Telemetry

  • Weight (4 load cells + HX711 on a separate scale frame with ant moats): best proxy for colony size, nectar flow and swarms.
  • Temperature/humidity: SHT45 in the brood box and one outside; a DS18B20 row across the frames maps the winter cluster.
  • Sound: INMP441 I2S mic, FFT on the ESP32, log band energies (not raw audio), including a 200-500 Hz dance-vibration experiment. Swarm/queenless classifier later, once there are labelled events from hive inspection notes.
  • Traffic: IR break-beam tunnels in phase 1; entrance camera in phase 2.

Pollen trap: be kind to the bees

  • Colonies get upset when pollen is taken, so the grid is servo-deployed only briefly, about hourly.
  • Retract once a pellet lands; skip runs when few bees are flying; log every deployment.
  • Fail open: gravity/spring holds the grid out, servo parks open on boot, a supercap parks it on brownout.

Pollen robot, design v2: catch, skewer, slice, swing, smear

Why v2: tape adhesive can't hold a pellet against a cutting wire (it tips and peels). Now the funnel throat and a skewer hold the pellet, so no cutting force goes through the tape. One sample takes 1-2 minutes, once an hour. 1. Funnel + throat: vibration feeds a PTFE-coated funnel; the ~4.5 mm throat holds one pellet on a gate flap; a break-beam confirms it. The pellet camera (white + 405 nm) logs colour, size and UV, and sets the skewer depth. 2. Skewer: a horizontal skewer wire feeds into one side, stopping 0.5 mm short of the cut plane (no carry-in contamination). 3. Cut: a vertical wire draw-cuts down the centre line, feeding while it cuts. 4. Drop: the gate opens and the free half lands on dry high-tack tape. That's the archive half, kept whole for DNA. 5. Pre-wet: a felt tip lays a short PG (or glycerin) + ethanol streak ahead of the stamp point. Index-matched clearing fluid. 6. Swing + smear: the arm pivots 90° so the cut face points down, then stamps/drags 2-3 smears at different pressures. That gives a density gradient, and software picks the best single-layer region. 7. Discard: the arm swings over a waste bin inside the cassette, a solenoid snips clean wire above the pellet, fresh wire feeds. 8. Laminate: cover film (0.13-0.17 mm, COC ideal) + a compliant pinch squashes the archive half into a disc. 9. Microscope (phase 2 bolt-on) scans the smear; the capstan meters tape by sprocket count.

  • Everything that touches pollen is single-use: fresh wire, fresh tape, bin leaves with the cassette. Run blank cycles to measure funnel/throat carryover.
  • Tape layout: archive disc (~5 mm) + smear zone (~10 mm) ≈ 23 mm = 3 sprocket frames per sample. Blank night gap of 3 frames (6 on Sundays) marks days, so only the cassette start/end date matters.
  • Wires: cut wire 35-38 AWG stainless at 2-7 N, ~25 mm per run; skewer ~0.3-0.4 mm spring stainless in a guide tube.
  • Dropped along the way: singulator disc, masking carrier tape, rolled print, syringe pump, twin-wire bridge, suction arm. Dry grinding is out (sugar binder smears, grains break).
  • Control: PLC-style firmware in C++ on the motion ESP32 (scan loop, step sequence, interlocks, timeouts, FAULT = grid open). OpenPLC/ladder as a side lab.
  • Pellet stickiness varies with humidity: log drawer RH, pellet size and press "hardness" per frame; 2-3 recipes like fuel trims.

Cassette

  • One cassette = one experiment run = 4-6 weeks, about one tagged cohort's foraging life. Tag the cohort ~3 weeks before loading.
  • Ships to a lab with a SQLite export (telemetry, focal-bee trips, per-pellet rows) keyed by cassette ID + frame.
  • Contents: sample tape, cover film, archive take-up, cut wire supply + used spool, skewer wire, felt-tip cartridge, waste bin, desiccant, QR run ID.
  • Tape: start with Scotch Crystal Clear 12 mm (glossy, acrylic adhesive); then forensic lifting tape. Cover: 16 mm clear polyester leader (sprocket indexing). Under $10 a season in consumables.
  • Size estimate: pollen unit ~15 × 12 × 6 cm, cassette ~13 × 8 × 2.5 cm, microscope bay ~12 × 4 × 4 cm.

Microscope (phase 2)

  • ~1 µm resolution: 20x/0.4 RMS objective + tube lens + lensless Pi camera (OpenFlexure-style), laid flat with a 45° mirror.
  • Depth of field ~3 µm: printed flexure Z (28BYJ-48 + M3 screw), 10-15 slice focus stacks. Tape advance does the X scan.
  • Brightfield LED below, oblique LEDs for texture, 405 nm for autofluorescence, permanent yellow long-pass filter.
  • Could first be a desktop cassette reader before it's docked into the hive unit.

Compute

  • ESP32-S3 Plus = hive node. Always on, mostly asleep: sensors, FFT, DS3231 RTC (time master), battery gauge, LoRa receive windows for bloom nodes, buffers readings, switches the Pi.
  • ESP32-S3 = motion controller for servos, steppers (TMC2209), LEDs; talks to the Pi over UART.
  • Pi Zero 2 W = imager and record keeper, power-gated per sample. SQLite master copy; read-only root + data partition; only cut power after a "halted" GPIO. Keep raw focus stacks (~10-25 GB a season).
  • Connectivity: send results and thumbnails only, over public Wi-Fi with an LTE-M fallback; the device dials out (WireGuard/Tailscale). Desktop syncs the DB for Grafana/Datasette graphs and model training.
  • Phase 2 adds a second Pi Zero 2 W + global-shutter camera for tags. Phase 3: my original Jetson Nano (bench, mains power).
  • "Ideal" later version: custom PCB (ESP32-S3 + RP2350 motion coprocessor), CM4, LiFePO4 + MPPT.

Pollen ID model

  • UV frame finds grain masks almost for free; UV brightness + size gates out debris.
  • One MobileNetV3-Small int8 on the best-focus crop + UV channels; a few seconds per sample on the Zero 2 W.
  • Pretrain on public datasets, fine-tune on my own tape reference set from local flowers. Count per pellet with a "mixed" flag; label tree (family > genus > type); unknowns get flagged for the lab. Model version on every row.

Power (Pacific Northwest)

  • ~3 Wh/day in season, ~0.5 Wh/day in winter mode (Nov-Feb): sensors every 15 min, counter and pollen off, one sync a day.
  • 2x 18650 (~20 Wh), bq24074 charger with NTC (no charging below 0 °C), MAX17048 fuel gauge.
  • 10 W panel on its own post, GX12 plug. The post doubles as the mount for a future phase 2 battery box (LiFePO4 + MPPT).

Next steps (bench season)

  1. Needle-skewer hand test: does ~1 mm of skewer hold through a thread cut? Is the hole visible on the cut face?
  2. Throat fit: do 2-4 mm pellets all sit centred, or does it need a V shape?
  3. Smear quality at 400x on the AmScope: PG/ethanol streak vs dry tape. Halos? Length of single layer?
  4. Clearing fluid: grain swelling at day 0, 7, 30; dry adhesive border seal on a wound spool.
  5. Blanks: full cycles with no pellet to measure carryover.
  6. Trap on a real hive in spring, with fresh pellets.

Open questions: hive type (Langstroth 8 or 10 frame?), Wi-Fi on site, stand height, frozen pellets for winter testing.
